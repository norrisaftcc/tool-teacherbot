## Systems Analysis Foundations & DataMan Discovery

Every successful information system begins before anyone writes production code. It begins by understanding the system that already exists, identifying what is known and what is assumed, and gathering enough evidence to decide what problem actually needs to be solved.

### Driving question

**How do you understand a system well enough to ask better questions before proposing a solution?**

### Why this module matters

Systems analysis and systems design are connected, but they are not the same work. Analysts examine people, processes, technology, needs, constraints, and evidence before a team commits to a solution. Designers use that understanding to shape how a solution should work.

In this module, you will build the foundation for both. You will practice noticing ambiguity, separating evidence from assumption, and reading an existing system closely enough to explain what it does before deciding what should change.

### The course case begins here

You will begin examining the **1977 Texas Instruments DataMan** and its documentation as source evidence. DataMan will remain a persistent course case. In this module, your job is not to redesign it yet. Your job is to understand what the existing system, its documentation, and its behavior can actually support.

### Module learning outcomes

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

**MLO 1.1 → CLO 1**

#### Explain systems analysis and design in context

Distinguish systems analysis from systems design and explain how people, processes, technology, and organizational needs shape information-system work.

**MLO 1.2 → CLO 1**

#### Identify ambiguity, assumptions, and evidence needs

Identify ambiguity, assumptions, sequence, and success criteria in problem descriptions and working instructions, and determine what additional evidence is needed before making a conclusion.

**MLO 1.3 → CLO 1**

#### Analyze an existing system from source evidence

Examine DataMan documentation and observed behavior to identify users, processes, constraints, system behavior, and unresolved questions without jumping prematurely to a solution.

Outcome

**CLO 1 — Analyze Systems Using Evidence.** Analyze an existing system, its users, processes, constraints, behavior, and available source evidence to identify relevant problems, assumptions, uncertainties, and opportunities for improvement.

### How your thinking will progress

**Observe**
What is here?

**Question**
What is unclear?

**Gather evidence**
What supports the claim?

**Conclude carefully**
What can you defend?

### What you will do

1. Learn the difference between systems analysis and systems design.
2. Examine how people, processes, and technology interact inside an information system.
3. Practice identifying ambiguity, hidden assumptions, missing steps, and unclear success criteria.
4. Use evidence before making claims about how a system works or what it needs.
5. Examine the Systems Development Life Cycle as a framework for how analysis, design, implementation, and evaluation connect.
6. Read the DataMan documentation as a systems analyst rather than as a casual user.
7. Carry your evidence and open questions forward into Module 2, where you will begin turning evidence and stakeholder needs into requirements.

Analyst rule

**Do not solve a problem you have not yet understood.** When evidence is incomplete, identify the gap. When instructions are ambiguous, expose the ambiguity. When a conclusion depends on an assumption, make the assumption visible.

**What should carry forward into Module 2?**

By the end of Module 1, you should have an evidence-based understanding of the existing DataMan system and enough systems-analysis practice to distinguish what you observed from what you assumed.

Module 2 begins from that evidence. The next question is no longer simply *“What does this system do?”* It becomes **“What should a modern system be required to do, and what evidence supports that requirement?”**

### Keep this question in front of you

**What do I actually know about this system—and what evidence lets me say that?**
