## Stakeholder Elicitation Under Pressure

You know that analysts should clarify vague requests before turning them into requirements. Now you will work with incomplete information, a limited investigation budget, and a new constraint that may force you to reconsider your first decision.

Outcome

**MLO 2.2 — Elicit and Clarify Needs:** Apply elicitation techniques to clarify ambiguous stakeholder statements and identify the need behind a request.

**Your role:** Systems Analyst for the DataMan modernization project.

### The Situation

Stakeholders want a modern version of DataMan, but their requests mix real needs, preferences, assumptions, and possible solutions. You will not have unlimited access to them. Your job is to decide which questions are worth asking, what the evidence actually supports, and what the team should refuse to assume.

Caution

You may choose only three investigation questions. Every choice uses part of your investigation budget, even if the answer turns out to be less useful than you hoped.

### THINK — Before You Launch

Do not try to predict a hidden correct path. Instead, begin by thinking like an analyst:

- Which stakeholder statements are still too vague to become requirements?
- Which unknowns could create the greatest project risk if the team guesses?
- Which question would give you evidence about a **need** rather than a preferred interface or technology?
- What information would help you distinguish a functional requirement from a non-functional requirement?

Analyst rule

**Keep this lens in mind:** Evidence → Need → Requirement. If you cannot defend one of those connections yet, document the uncertainty instead of inventing an answer.

### ACT — During the Simulation

1. **Choose three investigation questions.** You will not be able to ask everything.
2. **Read the evidence carefully.** Record what the evidence actually tells you, not what you hoped it would tell you.
3. **Separate evidence from assumptions.** Identify at least one thing you know and one thing you still do not know.
4. **Draft an initial requirements position.** Identify a likely functional requirement, a likely non-functional requirement or quality constraint, and an assumption or proposed solution that is not yet confirmed.
5. **Explain why your position is defensible.** Point back to the evidence you gathered.
6. **Respond to the complication.** When new project information appears, decide whether you should revise, keep, defer, or reopen part of your earlier decision.

### What to Pay Attention To

**Evidence**

What did the stakeholder or source actually establish?

**Uncertainty**

What is still unclear, unconfirmed, or risky to assume?

**Solution Bias**

Are you treating one possible interface or technology as though it were the requirement?

**Revision**

When new evidence appears, what should change and what is still defensible?

### REVIEW — Before You Save Anything

When the simulation reaches the decision-record stage, review your reasoning before you copy it. Your record should make it possible for another project team member to understand how you reached your decision.

- Can you identify the three questions you chose?
- Can you point to the evidence each useful question revealed?
- Did you clearly distinguish supported evidence from remaining uncertainty?
- Is your likely functional requirement tied to evidence?
- Is your likely non-functional requirement or quality constraint tied to evidence?
- Did you avoid treating an unsupported interface, database, or technology preference as a requirement?
- Did you explain how the new complication affected your initial position?

Analyst rule

**Different investigation paths can produce different defensible decisions.** The goal is not identical answers. The goal is a decision that follows from evidence and clearly identifies what remains uncertain.
